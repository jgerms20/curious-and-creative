About this episode

Europe came out of the Second World War and built a table: NATO, pooled coal and steel, eventually a shared history textbook between France and Germany. East Asia came out of the same war and built a wheel. The United States sits at the hub, and Japan, South Korea, the Philippines, and Taiwan each hold a separate spoke, facing Washington one at a time and rarely each other. This episode asks why. The answer is not that Asia's nations failed to organize. It is that the system was designed, in San Francisco in 1951, so they would not have to, and so Washington would never lose control of clients it feared might drag it into a larger war.

Key points

  • The United States sits at the hub, and Japan, South Korea, the Philippines, and Taiwan each hold a separate spoke, facing Washington one at a time and rarely each other.
  • We trace the founding bargain that kept Emperor Hirohito on his throne and parked war responsibility where it still sits, the apology for the atomic bombings that never came, and Okinawa, the island that keeps the alliance's bill.
  • We look at the one real attempt at an Asian NATO, SEATO, and why it dissolved while the bilateral spokes endured, at ASEAN's refusal to choose between Washington and Beijing, and at the latticework that stands in a pact's place today
  • the Quad, AUKUS, and a commitment to consult from Camp David.
  • When Japan's prime minister proposed an Asian NATO in 2024, Washington called it a fantasy within the week.
